# Nigerian professionals: job frustrations and EITDA content opportunities

Research and website content brief · 8 September 2026

Prepared for EITDA / Eshiet Foundation. Scope: employed professionals in Nigeria, drawing mainly on publications from 2024–2026 available by the research date. Reviewed against the current messaging in `app/page.tsx`. This is a desk study and proposed content direction; no audience interviews or website changes were undertaken.

**The recommended positioning**

EITDA should speak to the professional who has useful experience but cannot clearly see how to turn it into better career or earning options. The strongest starting points are financial pressure, demanding workloads, uncertainty about the next move, and the need for practical learning that builds on existing expertise.

Recommended message: **“You have experience. Build clearer options for what comes next.”**

The evidence supports acknowledging difficult working conditions. It does not establish that professionals lack ambition, that everyone wants to leave their profession, or that a digital-skills event will increase participants’ income. EITDA can credibly explain its learning process and show useful examples; employment and earnings outcomes require its own evidence.

**What the research establishes**

The findings below distinguish employee surveys, professional samples, household context and editorial interpretation. The order reflects relevance to EITDA, not a statistically measured ranking of frustrations. First-person lines are original proposed copy, not quotations or testimonials.

**1. Earning a salary does not necessarily feel like making progress.**

PwC’s Nigeria summary of its 2024 workforce survey reports that 72% rated fair pay very or extremely important, while 30% described a household financial position with enough left after bills for savings, holidays and extras. The Nigeria sample size is not disclosed in this short summary. [PwC Nigeria, *Six actions to build a future-fit workforce in Nigeria*, based on the 2024 survey](https://www.pwc.com/ng/en/assets/pdf/six-actions-to-build-a-future-fit-workforce-in-nigeria%20.pdf).

The World Bank’s April 2026 Nigeria Development Update adds current context: economic conditions had improved, but household incomes had not fully recovered. That is household-level analysis, not a measurement of professional salaries. [World Bank, *Nigeria Development Update*, April 2026](https://www.worldbank.org/en/country/nigeria/publication/nigeria-development-update-ndu).

EITDA implication: lead with the desire to make existing capability count. Show how a professional can identify a problem they solve, demonstrate their contribution and explore who values it. Avoid suggesting that low pay is simply a failure of personal branding.

Proposed copy: **“You are working. You are gaining experience. But the progress you hoped for still feels out of reach.”**

**2. There may be very little energy left to plan a better future.**

PwC’s 2024 Nigeria summary reports that 48% experienced a significant workload increase and 54% felt too much workplace change was happening at once. These describe the surveyed employees and the survey period, not every professional today. [PwC Nigeria, 2024 workforce findings](https://www.pwc.com/ng/en/assets/pdf/six-actions-to-build-a-future-fit-workforce-in-nigeria%20.pdf).

Gallup’s April 2026 report places Nigerian employee stress at 55% and engagement at 17%, using three-year rolling averages ending in 2025. Stress means experiencing it a lot during the previous day; it is not a burnout diagnosis or proof that work caused it. Engagement is a separate measure from job satisfaction. [Gallup, *Nigeria: State of the Global Workplace*, 2026 report](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/706562/state-global-workplace-nigeria-country-level-data.aspx).

EITDA implication: explain the practical steps participants will work through and the actual time required. A short, specific exercise—such as translating one work achievement into a case example—is more credible than an expansive promise to transform their career overnight. Avoid framing extra work as the universal answer to exhaustion.

Proposed copy: **“By the time work ends, there is little energy left to figure out your next move.”**

**3. Additional income is relevant, but the route needs to be concrete.**

ACCA’s 2025 Africa report shows that 44% of its Nigerian accountancy and finance respondents were undertaking another role. The regional survey covered 1,349 respondents in 45 countries; country results required at least 70 responses, but Nigeria’s exact sample is not supplied. Additional roles are not necessarily digital, profitable or driven solely by financial necessity. [ACCA, *Africa Talent Trends 2025*, pp. 4 and 12](https://www.accaglobal.com/content/dam/ACCA_Global/professional-insights/Global-talent-trends-2025/PI-GTT-2025-AFRICA%20v6.pdf).

EITDA implication: demonstrate the difference between possessing a skill and offering a defined service. A useful workshop example is turning financial-reporting experience into a clearly scoped monthly management-reporting offer, with a sample deliverable and an identified customer problem. Treat it as an offer to investigate, not proof of market demand or guaranteed work.

Proposed copy: **“You want another earning option. Start by understanding what you can already help someone do.”**

**4. Advancement can depend on access and institutional support as well as ability.**

WISCAR’s Women in Law assessment identifies barriers including gender bias, caregiving demands, weak institutional support and limited professional networks. Its April 2025 report includes an online survey of 428 primarily female legal professionals, administered from 15 March to 4 April 2025, alongside qualitative research. This is evidence about that legal-sector sample, not all Nigerian women or professions. The document is hosted in a March 2026 folder; its cover date remains April 2025. [WISCAR / FIDA, *Women in Law Mentoring Programme Needs Assessment*, April 2025](https://wiscar.ng/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Needs-Assessment-Report.pdf).

EITDA implication: make networking purposeful. Proposed activities could include explaining one's expertise to a relevant peer, receiving feedback on positioning and identifying a useful follow-up conversation. Only advertise structured introductions or mentoring once organisers have arranged them. Personal confidence alone cannot remove institutional barriers.

Proposed copy: **“You know your work. Finding the people and opportunities that recognise it can be harder.”**

**5. Experienced people can need new tools without being beginners in their profession.**

A 2025 study of university library professionals found gaps in digital skills for specific operational tasks. The full paper reports 408 usable questionnaires from 453 distributed across 12 southern Nigerian universities; the abstract's broader wording should not be mistaken for the analysed sample. The fieldwork dates were not established in this review. [Adetunla and Chowdhury, *Digital skills development of university library professionals in Nigeria*, published 11 March 2025](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/92535/1/Adetunla-and-Chowdhury-2025-Digital-skills-development-of-university-library-professionals-in-Nigeria.pdf).

ACCA's regional results also show concern about future skills and limited satisfaction with employer AI training. These are Africa-wide findings, not Nigeria-specific percentages. [ACCA, *Africa Talent Trends 2025*, p. 7](https://www.accaglobal.com/content/dam/ACCA_Global/professional-insights/Global-talent-trends-2025/PI-GTT-2025-AFRICA%20v6.pdf).

The World Bank's 2026 digital-skills blueprint describes demand across sectors and shortcomings in training alignment. Its document date is 30 June 2026 and disclosure date 18 August 2026. This review accessed the catalogue abstract, not the full blueprint. [World Bank, *From Learning to Livelihood: Digital Skills Development Blueprint 2026–2035*](https://documents.worldbank.org/pt/publication/documents-reports/documentdetail/099081826104041745).

EITDA implication: start with a recognisable work task, then show the additional digital capability needed. Explain prerequisites and illustrate a finished output. “Build on what you know” should still leave room for learning, practice and feedback.

Proposed copy: **“Your profession has taught you valuable things. Discover how to apply them as the tools and opportunities change.”**

**6. Work takes time and money beyond the hours on the job.**

Punch's 1 July 2026 coverage of NBS Transport Fare Watch reports an average intra-city bus fare of ₦1,431.25 per trip in May 2026, up 38.63% from May 2025. The NBS repository confirms the May report and its 29 June metadata date; the numeric report inside its ZIP was not directly extracted. These figures therefore remain secondary-reported NBS data, not a verified personal commuting budget. [Punch, *Average city bus fare rises*, 1 July 2026](https://punchng.com/average-city-bus-fare-rises-2-43-to-n1431-in-may/); [NBS, *Transport Fare Watch* repository](https://microdata.nigerianstat.gov.ng/index.php/catalog/161/related-materials).

CIPM's 2025 report, based on 1,704 HR practitioners, ranks work-life balance as the leading social issue influencing HR practice. It measures practitioner priorities, not the percentage of employees experiencing a problem. [CIPM, *HR Practice Needs Report 2025*, methodology and discussion of social issues](https://cipmnigeria.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/CIPM_E_HR-PRACTICE-NEEDS-REPORT_2025FINAL.pdf).

EITDA implication: publish clear start/end times, attendance modes and what each ticket actually includes. The existing free physical/virtual General Access is useful messaging. Do not imply all remote careers are suitable or that working from home removes every cost.

Proposed copy: **“Your next step needs to fit the life and responsibilities you already have.”**

**7. Exploring change can feel risky when other people rely on the same income.**

Piggyvest's 2025 report says six in ten respondents lacked emergency savings. Among respondents with income, 30% provided family support consistently and 33% occasionally. The study covered more than 26,000 people across all six geopolitical zones; it included people outside professional employment. Its public presentation does not establish a professionals-only result or provide enough detail here to verify national weighting. [Piggyvest, *Savings Report 2025*, publicly available by March 2026](https://www.piggyvest.com/reports/2025).

EITDA implication: present exploration as a sequence of practical decisions. Suggested exercises include identifying transferable experience, choosing one opportunity to investigate and testing an initial offer or application. Include current workload and responsibilities when evaluating that option. EITDA should not suggest resignation is a prerequisite to participation.

Proposed copy: **“You want better options—and a way to explore them while managing today's responsibilities.”**

**A finding that should shape the tone**

A Yola teaching-hospital study of 200 doctors and nurses found that 81% described heavy workloads, while 60% reported job satisfaction. Published online on 4 February 2026 in a 2025 journal issue, it is local cross-sectional evidence; fieldwork dates were not verified. It illustrates that valued work and difficult conditions can coexist, without establishing how many individuals belonged to both groups. [Noel et al., *Prevalence, Patterns, and Predictors of Burnout Among Healthcare Workers in a Tertiary Hospital in North-Eastern Nigeria*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13379833/).

PwC's 2025 Africa report also describes considerable meaning and optimism at work, with Nigeria among the countries reporting stronger career meaning. Its 1,753 responses cover five countries, with Nigeria accounting for 12%; pooled African findings should retain that label. [PwC, *Africa Workforce Hopes and Fears Survey 2025*, November report / December release](https://www.pwc.co.za/en/assets/pdf/africa-workforce-hopes-and-fears-survey-2025.pdf).

The editorial conclusion is to respect the reader's professional identity. Show how experience can support a next step. Dissatisfaction with compensation or working conditions does not mean their whole career was a mistake.

**Profession-specific examples to develop**

These are proposed demonstrations for EITDA. They are not verified vacancies, earnings forecasts or confirmed programme activities. Validate each with a practitioner and a potential employer/customer before publishing a detailed opportunity claim.

| Audience | Experience to surface | Possible demonstration | Evidence to show the visitor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Accountants and finance staff | Reconciliation, reporting, budgeting, explaining financial information | Turn a reporting task into a scoped service or an achievement-led job application | An anonymised reporting sample and a clear explanation of the business decision it supports |
| Teachers and trainers | Explaining concepts, lesson planning, assessment, feedback | Adapt a familiar lesson into a short digital learning module | Learning objective, sample lesson and assessment; check additional instructional-design skills needed |
| Healthcare professionals | Patient education, clinical workflows, documentation | Map an administrative workflow or develop an educational resource within the person's competence | A reviewed example using fictional data; distinguish it from delivering regulated clinical services |
| HR, administration and public-service professionals | Coordination, onboarding, record keeping, stakeholder communication | Build an onboarding workflow or operations case example | A process map, sample documents and a defined audience |
| Banking and customer-service staff | Customer onboarding, complaint resolution, explaining products | Translate service experience into a customer-support or operations portfolio example | A fictional customer journey with the problem, actions and proposed success measures |
| Librarians and information professionals | Information organisation, search, metadata and records | Demonstrate a task using relevant digital information-management tools | A searchable sample collection or documented workflow |

**Recommended homepage content**

Place a compact recognition section after the hero, followed by the existing “You don't have to start from zero” explanation. Its purpose is to help the reader recognise their situation before meeting the programme's three learning tracks.

Suggested section title: **“Experienced. Capable. Ready for more.”**

Suggested introduction:

> You have spent years solving problems, serving people and learning how your industry works. Yet your pay, progression or next opportunity may still feel uncertain. EITDA starts with that experience and helps you explore where it could take you.

Suggested cards:

| Card title | Body copy |
| --- | --- |
| Your pay feels stretched | You are putting in the work, but the financial progress you hoped for still feels far away. |
| Your next move feels unclear | You have experience. You want to understand which opportunities it prepares you for. |
| You want to build on what you know | You are ready to learn new tools and apply your expertise in new ways. |
| You need a practical starting point | You want to understand who needs your skills, what you can offer and how to take the first step. |

Suggested bridge to the programme:

> Find your value. Package what you know. Take it to market. Explore a clearer role, service or direction built around the experience you already have.

Suggested primary CTA: **“Explore the programme”** linking to `#programme`; retain **“Choose your ticket”** for registration. These use the site's existing destinations.

Keep the homepage centred on people and practical examples. If an evidence note is added, use a single accurately scoped statistic with its year and a nearby source link, rather than a row of alarming percentages. The full definitions belong in a supporting research article.

**Changes recommended for the existing page**

The current hero addresses professionals, business owners and workers. The “Who is the programme for?” FAQ describes curious young people, while the experience FAQ foregrounds beginners. Align those answers so an experienced applicant can recognise themselves.

Proposed audience FAQ:

> EITDA is for professionals, workers, business owners and people exploring their next step. It is especially relevant if you want to understand how your existing knowledge and experience can contribute to new roles, services or business opportunities in the digital economy.

Proposed experience FAQ:

> You do not need a technology background to attend. EITDA begins with your existing experience and helps you explore what you can build on and what you may need to learn next.

This wording follows the site's current beginner-accessible positioning. Organisers should substantiate any more specific prerequisites before adding them.

Retain the three existing tracks. Strengthen each with a concrete worked example. Consider wording learning outcomes as “Work towards a clearer offer and next step” until the delivery format supports the current unconditional “You will know” statements. When speaker announcements are ready, show how their experience relates to participants' actual work and decisions.

**Six feature articles worth commissioning**

| Priority | Proposed title | Reporting or practical material required | EITDA connection |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Employed, experienced—and still trying to move forward | Interview professionals about the gap between their expectations and present options | Introduce the event's purpose through real, consented experiences |
| 2 | What could someone pay you to help them do? | Three practitioner-reviewed examples, each with a problem, audience and sample output | Make “Package What You Know” concrete |
| 3 | What your job has taught you beyond your job title | Skill-mapping examples across finance, teaching, healthcare and operations | Support “Find Your Value” |
| 4 | Planning your next move around a demanding job | A realistic time-budget example and an interview about constraints | Show respect for workload and family responsibilities |
| 5 | New tools, familiar expertise | Demonstrate one occupational task before and after learning a relevant tool | Explain learning as an extension of professional capability |
| 6 | The real cost of getting to work | Dated commute diaries from Ikot Abasi/Uyo and at least one other city | Explore flexibility and access with locally relevant evidence |

These are article briefs, not completed reported stories. Avoid invented characters presented as real people, unattributed quotations, unverified salary gains or presenting 2025 event photographs as evidence of subsequent earnings.

**What to validate with EITDA's own audience**

The strongest remaining gap is local fit. This review did not establish representative frustration rankings for professionals in Ikot Abasi, Uyo or Akwa Ibom. A practical next research step is 12–15 short interviews spanning professions, career stages and caregiving responsibilities, with a substantial local group. This would be qualitative validation, not a national survey.

Ask what currently feels most limiting; what better work would look like; what they have already tried; what prevents another step; which work achievements they can demonstrate; and what a useful event would need to help them do. Let interviewees introduce frustrations before suggesting answers. Separately obtain permission for any name, photograph or quotation used publicly.

Test the draft recognition section with those readers. Check whether it feels accurate, whether the programme seems relevant to their profession, and whether they can explain its value after reading it. Measure useful outcomes after the event—such as completed offers, portfolio examples or relevant follow-ups—before making income-impact claims.

**Evidence limits and research coverage**

This study searched current Nigerian workforce evidence, professional-body surveys, household financial context, transport reporting, and profession-specific studies. Follow-up checks focused on original sources, sample definitions, publication versus fieldwork dates, and findings that complicated an overly negative story.

It does not prove that digital work pays more for every profession; that courses cause salary growth; that Nigerian professionals generally want to resign; or that particular proposed services have customers ready to buy. No robust national rate for professional career stagnation, toxic management or fear of layoffs was established. Those remain topics for carefully framed interviews, not headline statistics.

Several recent documents contain older observations. Gallup's latest publication uses pooled data through 2025. Some occupational studies do not establish fieldwork dates in the material reviewed. WISCAR's follow-up PDF retrieval timed out; its cover, indexed survey description and executive-summary evidence were accessible. The World Bank blueprint was assessed through its catalogue abstract. Transport amounts were checked in attributed reporting alongside the official catalogue, not inside the primary ZIP.

Research stopped when the principal content themes had supporting evidence and the remaining gaps mainly required original audience research or programme validation. Additional broad searching would not establish whether these messages resonate with EITDA's own prospective attendees.
